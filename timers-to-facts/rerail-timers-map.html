Rerail's timers: where the software waits on a clock, and what we do about it

Rerail 0.1.5 has 383 places where time passing decides something, and in the product a clock, not a fact, fires 68 of its 93 changes of state. 171 can go (86 so that a person waits less or learns sooner, 85 with no change a person notices), 4 are already being removed, 15 need one run to settle, and 193 stay, each documented with the harm its removal would do. We remove them in 71 changes ranked by what a person gets back, the product before the tests: the worst, a hook that waits 12 s on a daemon that does not answer (60.2 s on Windows when every pipe is taken) and loses its event, drops to about 0.2 s for every hook after the first, with the event kept; 6 changes are being built today.

The state maps

Each machine of the software, drawn as its states (the columns) and every change between them (one arrow per row). A blue solid arrow is fired by a fact: an event, a command, an exit. An orange dashed arrow with a clock mark is fired by a clock; it carries its change id, the clock's values and the timers there, and a mark for what it becomes: a fact in full, a fact with one bound left for a party that may never answer, a clock that stays, or a run needed to decide. The table under each drawing says what each timer guards and what replaces it.

How to read the ids. A timer id names one site in the code: A for the hooks, the daemon and their scripts; B for the commands, set-up, the dashboard and the engine's TypeScript side; C for the engine in Rust; D for the tests. A group of timers removed together by one change carries the same letter with G (AG11, BG01, CG1, DG1). A change of state has an id from its machine: CK a checkout, DM the daemon, SE a session, HE a hook event, ST the content store, DB the Rerail database, CM a command, DA the dashboard and status displays, CI a contributor or CI run, TS a test. Pull requests are named by their number.

The ten categories of use

Every timer site falls in one of ten patterns. Each pattern has one practice that replaces the clock with a fact on Linux, macOS and Windows, and the plan removes each pattern's sites by groups.

A wait for another process to come up or go away

The pattern, one real case. src/runtime/daemon-control.ts:1152-1168 (readyDaemon), START_TIMEOUT_MS 15 s, POLL_INTERVAL_MS 100 ms: 15 s limit, 100 ms poll, constants. It guards the launched daemon to write daemon.json naming its pid and answer its probe. Who waits: SessionStart hook when no daemon runs; person at rerail daemon start, set-up, update, every daemon launch.

Wrong states and waits today. A start that is still going is reported failed ('did not become ready within 15s') while the daemon comes up anyway on a slow machine; a dead daemon or registry is noticed only at the next poll, or at the limit; a restart at the heap ceiling waits a fixed 1 s for its predecessor and leaves no daemon when that one takes longer. Worst: 15 s and a false failure at rerail daemon start on a slow machine (A109); up to 179 s per CI run whose local registry never started (A815, A828); about 45 min per Windows CI machine whose test run died (A844).

The practice that replaces it
Wait on the process itself. Coming up: the child writes one ready line on a pipe its parent passed it, once it listens; the parent reads that line or the pipe's end (its exit), whichever comes first. Going away: the process handle. Linux: pidfd_open and poll (kernel 5.3 and later), or waitpid for one's own child. macOS: kqueue with EVFILT_PROC and NOTE_EXIT. Windows: WaitForSingleObject, or RegisterWaitForSingleObject on the handle the spawn kept, so no thread blocks. In Node: the 'exit' event of a child it started. A kill grace stays, because nothing says in advance whether a process will obey SIGTERM.

A wait for a lock

The pattern, one real case. native/src/content_store/connection.rs:17 (used 100-106, 121-171; OperationDeadline 78-97): 10 s per operation (all writes of one apply, attach or completion share it). It guards a store write gives up (StoreError::Busy) once it has waited 10 s in all on other writers. Who waits: a coding agent's tool call through a hook: call, result and turn-end wait for the code-rule apply (runtime.ts:209, 1011), and the apply publishes to the store before it answers (workspace/api.rs:1040-1180); also the store attach at a session's first check (api.rs:777-830), each publishing apply that meets another writer: in-process writers outside the evaluation turn are the Git snapshot publication of every workspace attach, the maintenance wake every 30 s, and completion tasks; on a checkout's own store, a second process.

Wrong states and waits today. A newcomer can take a just-released lock before an older waiter; a checked edit behind a background writer of the content store waits for the writer's whole run, not one batch, because every gap goes to the writer that polls fastest; a record write on the thread that answers hooks waits on another process's transaction and holds every hook behind it. Worst: Up to 10 s per checked edit while another writer keeps the content store (C01; measured 1.7 to 3.4 s behind batch runs of 20 to 200 ms, against 8.8 to 104.8 ms with a first-come handoff); 5 s per record write, stacking to the hook's 12 s, while reset, scrub or an upgrade holds the database (B212); 15 s on the start lock (A107).

The practice that replaces it
In one process: a first-come queue (a mutex and condition variable that hands the lock to the next waiter), so a waiter parks until the holder's commit and is served in arrival order. Across processes: an OS lock the system frees when its holder ends. Linux and macOS: flock with LOCK_EX and without LOCK_NB, in a worker thread (or open file description locks). Windows: LockFileEx without LOCKFILE_FAIL_IMMEDIATELY on an overlapped handle; CancelIoEx ends a wait the caller gave up. For SQLite across processes there is no release event: keep the busy timeout, but take the waiting off the thread that answers hooks (a writer thread) and route long writes through the one process that owns the file.

A poll for a file, a record or a change

Wrong states and waits today. Wrong states and waits today. A busy daemon is taken for a dead one: a hook gives up at 12 s and loses its event, an MCP call says 'daemon is not running' at 2 s, a Windows lookup deletes the live daemon's record. How it was made

  • Four audits by area, at Rerail 0.1.5 (commit 9cad73564), one row per timer site read in the code: what it waits for, its value and where it is set, who waits and how often, the longest wait, and a verdict with its reason.
  • A, hooks and daemon. 135 sites, one row each, from 114 files read line by line.
  • B, commands, set-up and dashboard. 117 sites, one row each, from 95 files read line by line.
  • C, the engine in Rust. 47 sites, one row each, from 5 files read line by line.
  • D, tests. 84 sites, one row each.
  • Then the map: each row placed on the links and the changes of state it sits on, the 101 changes classed by what fires them, and each category given the practice that replaces it. 5 sites were traced by two audits and count once.
  • Measured, not read: 1 timing model of the content store's write wait, on a Linux machine. Every other figure is read from the code, or from a measurement the row names.

Built from the audits and the map by a script that checks every id on a drawing against the audits and every total against the map. Figures are read from the code of Rerail 0.1.5 unless a row says it was measured.
